Welcome & what you'll need

Before we touch paint, a quick tour of where we're going. This course exists so your first proper painting session doesn't start with an hour of confusion in an art shop. By the end you'll own fewer than ten things, and every one of them will earn its place.

Don't buy anything yet — watch the whole course first, then use the checklist below. Most students already own half of it.

Crystal's note ✦Cheap paint on good paper beats good paint on cheap paper. Every single time. If you only upgrade one thing, upgrade the paper.

Choosing paper — weight, texture, sizing

Paper is the instrument; paint is just the breath. We look at why 300gsm cold-press is the safe default, what "sizing" actually does, and how to spot a pad that will buckle the moment it sees water.

We test three papers on camera — supermarket cartridge, student cold-press, and one expensive sheet — so you can see exactly what the extra money buys (and when it buys nothing).

Crystal's note ✦Tape your paper down on all four sides before wetting it. The 30 seconds of taping saves the whole painting later.

Brushes that matter (and ones that don't)

You need three brushes. The art shop will sell you thirty. We cover what a round no.8 can do on its own — lines, washes, dry-brush — and why a good point matters more than natural hair at this stage.

Also: how to murder a brush (leaving it tip-down in the jar), and how to make a £6 synthetic last three years.

Crystal's note ✦Test a round brush before buying: wet it, flick it once. If it snaps back to a point, it's yours. If it splays, walk away.

A starter palette of 8 colours

Eight tubes mix almost everything you'll paint in your first year: two blues, two yellows, two reds, two earths. We swatch all eight, then mix greens, greys, and skin tones live so you see the range hiding in so few colours.

Why no black and no white? Because mixing your darks teaches colour faster than any exercise I know — and the paper is your white.

Crystal's note ✦Buy artist quality for the two blues if you can stretch — blue does the most lifting in watercolour, and the cheap ones go chalky.

Setting up your workspace & water

A kitchen table is a perfectly good studio. We set one up from scratch: board angle (about 15°), light from the non-dominant side, the two-jar water system, and where the kitchen roll lives so your reflex reaches it without looking.

Small rituals matter — a tidy setup means the moment inspiration arrives, nothing stands between you and the first wash.

Crystal's note ✦Two jars, always: one "wash" jar for dirty rinsing, one "clean" jar for picking up fresh water. Muddy water makes muddy paintings.

Your first wash — let's test everything

The graduation piece: a flat wash, a graded wash, and a simple two-colour sky. Nothing to frame — everything to learn. You'll feel how much water the brush holds, how the paper drinks, how the bead of paint travels.

Paint along with me in real time. When your sky dries with a soft gradient and no cauliflower blooms, you're ready for any beginner course in the catalog.

Crystal's note ✦Mix more paint than you think you need. Running out mid-wash is the most common beginner heartbreak — and the easiest to avoid.
